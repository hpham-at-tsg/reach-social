Post 1 · Tue Dec 8, 11:30 AM · Seasonal tips
Holiday shopping for a little one? A few quick checks before you buy.

1. Read the label. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that age recommendations help you gauge choking hazards and whether a toy matches a child's needs and interests at their stage of development. For many kids in therapy, stage matters as much as age.

2. Think large. The AAP advises that all toys and parts be larger than your child's mouth.

3. Be careful with toys that use button or lithium coin batteries or high-powered magnets. If they come loose, young children may put them in their mouths, noses, or ears, which can cause serious injuries.

4. Give the gift of movement. Ride-ons, balls, balance toys, and active games can be great picks. Not sure what fits? Ask your physical therapist for ideas matched to how your child plays and moves right now.

Save this for your shopping list.

Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, "How to Buy Safe Toys."

Post 3 · Tue Dec 22, 11:30 AM · Research spotlight (kids)
Does practice really help kids who find movement skills harder to learn? A new review says yes.

Researchers pooled school-based motor skill programs for children and teens (ages 5 to 19) with developmental coordination disorder (DCD) or probable DCD. Across 18 studies with a comparison group, the programs had a large positive effect on motor performance. The biggest gains came with practice at least 3 times a week and at least 500 minutes in total.

The takeaway for families: regular, frequent practice matters. Winter break is a great time to keep skills going at home with short, playful sessions a few times a week.

Not sure which skills to focus on? Ask your physiatrist, your physical therapist, or your child's school team.

Save this for winter break.

Source: Pohjanvirta A, et al. Arch Phys Med Rehabil. 2026;107(11):3385-3395.

Post 4 · Tue Dec 29, 11:30 AM · Research spotlight (adults)
Months after ACL surgery and your knee still isn't right? You're not out of options.

A new randomized trial in Annals of Internal Medicine followed 184 young adults (ages 18 to 40) who still had knee problems 9 to 36 months after ACL reconstruction. Half did a 4-month, twice-weekly exercise therapy program supervised by a physiotherapist, with education. The other half had a single education session and a self-directed exercise resource.

At 4 months, the supervised group improved more on knee symptoms and function, gained more strength, and more often reported their pain was better (73% vs 40%). By 12 months the gap had narrowed as the other group kept improving, while the supervised group's gains held.

The takeaway: persistent knee problems after ACL surgery can still improve with a structured exercise program. Planning a fresh start in the new year? Ask your physiatrist or physical therapist what fits you.

Share this with someone who's been there.

Source: Culvenor AG, et al. Ann Intern Med. 2026;179(11):1525-1534.
